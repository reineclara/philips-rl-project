\section{Method}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section formalizes the contract-coverage optimization problem as a
Markov decision process (MDP) and describes the reinforcement-learning
algorithm we adopt, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).


\subsection{MDP formulation}
\label{subsec:mdp}

We model the problem as a finite-horizon MDP in which the state describes
the contractual and operational posture of the managed equipment, the actions
correspond to coverage decisions, transitions capture failure, renewal and
aging dynamics, and the reward balances operational costs against the
consequences of non-coverage. Time is discretized on a monthly grid and
decisions are taken on the full set of managed equipment.


\paragraph{State space.}
At each time step, the state describes the contractual and operational
posture of the portfolio as a whole. For each piece of equipment, the local
state combines the current coverage type (no coverage, manufacturer warranty,
warranty extension, or maintenance contract), the time remaining before
expiration of the current coverage, the age of the equipment, its cumulative
usage, the recent history of interventions and failures, and static
attributes such as modality, clinical criticality, site of installation and
risk class. The global state aggregates these individual descriptions and
adds the residual contractual budget available to the organization.


\paragraph{Action space.}
At each time step, the agent chooses, for each piece of equipment, a coverage
decision drawn from a finite set: maintain the current situation, subscribe
to a new coverage, renew the existing coverage, raise the service level,
lower the service level, or terminate the ongoing coverage. Because the joint
action space is combinatorial, we restrict it to feasible decisions filtered
by contractual constraints such as eligibility dates, exclusivities and
available budget. To keep the dimension tractable, the policy is factorized
per piece of equipment, each being assigned a decision conditioned on the
global state.


\paragraph{Reward function.}
The reward aggregates three components. First, the expected service revenue
associated with active contracts on the installed base. Second, the
contractual costs incurred by coverage decisions. Third, two penalties: one
applied per equipment that remains uncovered, and one applied to failures
occurring while no active coverage is in place. The weighting coefficients
of these penalties are calibrated on historical data to reflect the
operational and financial priorities of the organization. The agent's
objective is to maximize the expected discounted return over the decision
horizon.


\paragraph{Transition dynamics.}
The transition between consecutive time steps decomposes into four
sub-processes that are independent conditionally on the chosen action.
The first reflects contractual evolution: the coverage type and the remaining
time before expiration are updated according to the chosen decision and time
decay, with automatic transition to the uncovered state when a contract
expires without renewal. The second reflects aging and usage: age is
incremented at each step and cumulative usage evolves according to a class-
specific distribution. The third models failures: their probability is
learned from intervention histories and conditions the update of the recent
history. The last tracks the contractual budget, reduced by the costs
incurred at each step and periodically replenished at the beginning of the
fiscal period. This decomposition supports estimating the components of the
dynamics separately and instantiating a reproducible simulator that respects
the Markov property on the augmented state.


\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)}
\label{subsec:ppo}

To solve the MDP defined in Section~\ref{subsec:mdp}, we use the \emph{Proximal
Policy Optimization} (PPO) algorithm~\cite{schulman2017ppo}, a policy-gradient
method recognized for its empirical stability and implementation simplicity,
and particularly suited to the large-dimensional discrete action spaces
considered here.


\paragraph{Principle.}
PPO belongs to the family of actor-critic methods. The algorithm jointly
trains two components: a stochastic policy, which maps each state to a
probability distribution over admissible actions, and a value function, which
estimates the expected future return from a given state. Both components are
parameterized by neural networks and learned from trajectories collected
under the current policy. The advantage estimate, which measures the marginal
gain of an action relative to the average value of the state, is obtained
through the GAE estimator~\cite{schulman2016gae}, which provides a
controllable trade-off between bias and variance.


\paragraph{Clipped objective.}
The specificity of PPO lies in its learning objective. From the collected
trajectories, the algorithm builds a surrogate objective comparing the
current policy to the policy that produced the data. To prevent overly large
updates likely to destabilize learning, this objective is \emph{clipped}:
excessively large policy changes are truncated, which guarantees that the
optimized policy remains close to the behavior policy. This implicit
constraint gives PPO its characteristic stability. The value function is
trained by minimizing the squared return-prediction error, and an entropy
bonus is added to the global objective to encourage exploration and to
prevent premature convergence to a deterministic policy.


\paragraph{Application to the coverage problem.}
The policy is instantiated in factorized form per piece of equipment. A
shared encoder produces an internal representation of each local state, and
an independent decision head per equipment outputs the distribution over
admissible actions. The critic shares the same encoder and aggregates the
local representations via pooling before predicting the global state value.
Contractual and budgetary constraints are enforced by \emph{masking}
infeasible actions before the softmax, which preserves the validity of the
policy while restricting exploration to admissible decisions. Training is
conducted offline on the simulator described in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, which enables intensive exploration without
operational risk and yields a reproducible evaluation of the learned
policies prior to any deployment.
