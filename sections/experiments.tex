\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section describes the simulated environment, the baselines, the training
setup and the evaluation protocol, and reports the empirical results.


\subsection{Dataset and simulation environment}
\label{subsec:dataset}

Direct online access to Philips France's contractual inventory is subject to
confidentiality and operational constraints, so we do not conduct training
on the production systems. Instead, we build a \emph{simulation environment}
whose parameter distributions are calibrated to reproduce the salient
characteristics of the real installed base: coverage levels, contract
durations, contractual costs and equipment risk levels. This environment
plays the role of both dataset and interaction simulator, which guarantees
experiment reproducibility and avoids any exposure of sensitive data.

Each episode represents a single piece of medical equipment tracked over a
finite horizon of $T$ daily time steps. At reset, the environment randomly
draws: a remaining time before current coverage expiration within a fixed
interval, a contract-cost level with three modalities (low, medium, high),
and a risk level with three modalities (low, medium, high). The equipment
is initially covered.

The state space is a four-dimensional vector combining the coverage status,
the number of days before expiration of the current coverage, the
contract-cost level and the risk level of the equipment. All components are
normalized to the unit interval before being fed to the policy.

The action space is discrete and contains three decisions: do nothing, renew
the contract, or extend the warranty. Renewal resets coverage to a fixed
duration, whereas warranty extension adds an extra duration at a reduced
cost, conditional on an active contract being in place.

The dynamics decrement the remaining time before expiration by one day at
each step. Expiration triggers a transition to the uncovered state and a
one-shot penalty. Failures are sampled at each step with a probability
increasing with the risk level; failures occurring on uncovered equipment
are penalized more heavily than others. The reward function aggregates six
components: a continuous coverage bonus, a bonus for renewals performed close
to expiration, a renewal or extension cost indexed on the contract-cost
level, a penalty for overly early renewals, a continuous non-coverage
penalty, and a one-shot expiration penalty. Coefficients are chosen so that
the per-step reward remains in a numerically stable range while preserving a
clear ordering between desirable and undesirable actions.


\subsection{Baseline policies}
\label{subsec:baselines}

We compare the PPO agent with two baseline policies. The first is a uniformly
random policy over the three actions, which sets a lower performance bound.
The second is a rule-based heuristic reproducing the logic commonly observed
in operational teams: if the equipment is uncovered, perform a renewal;
otherwise, if the contract is about to expire within a few days, trigger a
warranty extension; otherwise, take no action. This baseline reflects the
level of performance achievable with simple business rules and provides a
reference point to quantify the added value of learning.


\subsection{Architecture and training setup}
\label{subsec:training}

The policy and value function share a two-layer fully connected encoder of
width $64$ with $\tanh$ activations, followed by two linear heads: a softmax
head over the three actions and a scalar value head. Observations are
normalized online through a running estimate of the mean and variance (using
Welford's algorithm), then clipped to the interval $[-5, 5]$ before being
fed to the network.

Training uses PPO with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). At each
iteration, a full episode is collected under the current policy, the
advantage is computed, and the policy-value pair is updated for four passes
over mini-batches of size $64$. Advantages are normalized per batch. The
objective combines PPO's clipped loss, a squared value loss and an entropy
bonus encouraging exploration.

Hyperparameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hparams}. They follow
standard PPO values, with a slightly higher entropy coefficient to maintain
exploration over the considered training horizon.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{subsec:eval-protocol}

Every $100$ training episodes, the current policy is evaluated in
deterministic mode (argmax action selection) over $20$ episodes sharing
fixed simulation seeds. The best policy in terms of evaluation reward is
retained and restored at the end of training.

The final evaluation and the comparison with the baseline policies are
conducted on the \emph{same} $20$ simulation seeds, so that the three agents
face identical contract and failure trajectories. This procedure neutralizes
the stochastic variance of the environment and enables a direct comparison
between the learned policy, the heuristic policy and the random policy.

Three metrics summarize the performance of a policy on the evaluation set:
the \emph{mean cumulative reward} per episode, which aggregates all reward
components and is to be maximized; the \emph{mean number of uncovered steps},
which measures the time the equipment remains without active coverage and
is to be minimized; and the \emph{mean number of uncovered failures}, which
quantifies costly events that occur outside any contract, whose minimization
is an operational priority.


\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:results}

The performance of the three policies is reported in Table~\ref{tab:results}.
To reduce the variance of the estimates, every metric is averaged over $20$
independent evaluation episodes, conducted on the same simulation seeds to
guarantee a fair comparison across policies.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & Random & Heuristic & PPO \\
\midrule
Mean reward                           & $-184.91$ & $60.56$ & $55.08$ \\
Mean uncovered steps                  & $0.00$    & $0.00$  & $0.40$  \\
Mean uncovered failures               & $0.00$    & $0.00$  & $0.05$  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Performance comparison of the three policies over $20$ evaluation
         episodes (same seeds).}
\label{tab:results}
\end{table}

The random policy obtains a strongly negative reward despite a nearly
continuous coverage ($0.00$ uncovered steps on average). This apparently
counter-intuitive result stems from the structure of the action space:
by drawing uniformly among the three decisions, two of which---renew and
extend---preserve coverage, the random agent mechanically keeps contracts
active but accumulates prohibitive contractual costs. The absence of a
strategy thus manifests here as expensive over-coverage rather than
under-coverage, and confirms that the sole metric of uncovered steps does
not fully capture the quality of a policy.

The PPO agent reaches a high positive reward and demonstrates its ability
to learn, from interactions with the simulator alone, a policy that keeps
the equipment almost continuously covered ($0.40$ uncovered step and
$0.05$ uncovered failure per episode on average) while limiting renewal
costs.

The heuristic policy nonetheless slightly outperforms PPO. Three factors
explain this residual gap. First, the considered environment admits a
near-optimal policy expressible as a simple deterministic rule---renew when
coverage approaches expiration---which the heuristic encodes exactly.
Second, the four-dimensional state representation restricts the agent's
ability to exploit richer interactions (portfolio context, intervention
history, temporal dependencies) where RL would learn an advantage that no
simple rule can capture. Third, the training budget of $3{,}000$ episodes
brings the agent to a stable plateau but does not guarantee convergence to
the global optimum; longer training or richer observations would reduce the
residual gap.

These results are obtained in a deliberately simplified simulation
environment whose distributions are calibrated from a limited number of
parameters. They therefore do not directly entail the performance
achievable in production, where additional factors---portfolio
heterogeneity, global budget constraints, partial observability---that are
absent from the present framework come into play.

In this setting, reinforcement learning constitutes a relevant approach
complementary to business rules, whose advantage grows as the environment
gains in complexity and variability, conditions under which heuristics
become difficult to maintain and adapt.
